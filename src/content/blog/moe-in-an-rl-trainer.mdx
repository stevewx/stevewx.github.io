A Mixture-of-Experts model is a strange object to hold in your head: it can have
hundreds of billions of parameters yet spend, per token, about as few FLOPs as a
model a fraction of its size. That trade is exactly why MoE is everywhere in
pretraining. But an RL post-training pipeline (PPO, GRPO, RLOO — the policy-gradient
family) was mostly designed around *dense* models, and the sparsity that made
pretraining cheap quietly violates several of its assumptions at once. This post
walks the dense FFN to its sparse replacement, then through the four places it
bites once you wire it into an RL trainer.

## From one dense FFN to many sparse experts

In a standard transformer block, each token passes through a single
feed-forward network — one big shared matrix multiply that every token sees. A
sparse MoE layer replaces that one FFN with $N$ parallel **expert** FFNs plus a
small learned **router**. The router scores the experts for each token and sends
the token to only the top-$k$ of them (often $k=1$ as in Switch Transformer, or
$k=2$ as in Mixtral 8x7B).

Concretely, for a token hidden state $x$, the router produces gate logits, takes
a softmax over the selected experts, and combines their outputs:

$$
g(x) = \mathrm{softmax}\big(\mathrm{TopK}(W_r\, x,\; k)\big),
\qquad
y = \sum_{i \in \mathrm{TopK}} g_i(x)\, E_i(x)
$$

This displayed form is the Mixtral-style convention — select the top-$k$ logits,
then softmax over just those. Other designs differ: Switch Transformer softmaxes
over all $N$ experts first and then takes the top-1. The accounting that follows
is the whole point. **Total** parameters scale with $N$ — every expert is a full
FFN sitting in memory. **Active** parameters per token scale with $k$. With
$N=8$ experts and $k=2$, you store eight FFNs but compute two per token:

$$
P_{\text{total}} \sim N \cdot P_{\text{expert}},
\qquad
P_{\text{active}} \sim k \cdot P_{\text{expert}},
\qquad
\frac{P_{\text{active}}}{P_{\text{total}}} \approx \frac{k}{N}
$$

The ratio is $k/N$ counting only the expert FFNs; the whole-model active
fraction runs a bit higher, since attention, the router, embeddings, and any
shared always-on experts are active for every token — and, except for the tiny
$d \times N$ router gate, don't grow with $N$ either (Mixtral lands at roughly
13B active / 47B total, not exactly $2/8$). Either way, total params explode
while per-token FLOPs stay modest. That decoupling is the gift in pretraining —
and the bill comes due everywhere downstream.

## Break #1: memory, and why expert parallelism is mandatory

The first thing that breaks is the most basic: **fitting the model**.

Per-token compute is small, but the *weights* are not. Every expert must live in
GPU memory regardless of how rarely it fires. A naive data-parallel setup
replicates the entire model on every GPU — which for a large MoE means each GPU
must hold all $N$ experts of every layer. With total params in the hundreds of
billions, that OOMs immediately.

The standard answer is **expert parallelism (EP)**: shard the experts of a layer
*across* GPUs so each device owns only $N / \text{EP}$ of them. No single GPU
holds the full expert set. The catch is that routing now crosses device
boundaries. A token on GPU 0 whose top-$k$ includes an expert on GPU 3 has to be
*sent there* and its result sent back. That is an **all-to-all** collective,
twice per MoE layer (dispatch and combine):

$$
\text{tokens} \xrightarrow[\text{dispatch}]{\text{all-to-all}}
\text{experts on remote GPUs}
\xrightarrow[\text{combine}]{\text{all-to-all}}
\text{tokens}
$$

This communication does not exist in a dense model at all. In an RL trainer it
can show up *twice over*: once in the policy network you are training, and again
in the rollout engine generating trajectories *if* it too shards experts across
GPUs (it might instead use tensor parallelism, or fit the experts without expert
parallelism at all) — which is the next problem.

## Break #2: the rollout engine has to speak MoE too

RL post-training has a generation phase: the current policy autoregressively
produces completions that get scored and turned into gradient signal. That
generation almost always runs on a dedicated inference engine (vLLM, SGLang),
*not* the training framework.

For a dense model the engine just needs to run the architecture fast. For an MoE
it must implement the **same routing semantics, with an expert layout correctly
mapped from the trainer's** — the physical sharding can differ (a different EP
degree, or tensor parallelism instead), but the logical expert numbering has to
reconcile during weight conversion, or the engine runs a silently different
model. Two requirements that are trivial for dense models become load-bearing
here:

- The inference engine needs an MoE-aware kernel path: top-$k$ routing, expert
  dispatch, and its own all-to-all if it shards experts across inference GPUs.
- The **train-side and inference-side expert layouts must agree** so that
  checkpoint conversion is correct.

That last point is a classic source of subtle bugs. If the trainer numbers and
shards experts one way and the inference engine expects another, weight sync
will quietly map expert $i$'s parameters onto expert $j$'s slot. Nothing crashes
— the router still routes, the FFNs still multiply — but the policy you generate
with is no longer the policy you trained. Off-by-one expert permutations are
invisible to shape checks and lethal to learning.

```python
# illustrative — not a real implementation
# sync trained weights into the rollout engine, expert-aware
for layer in policy.moe_layers:
    for i, expert in enumerate(layer.experts):
        global_id = layer.local_to_global_expert_id(i)   # MUST match engine's map
        engine.load_expert(layer.idx, global_id, expert.state_dict())
# a wrong local_to_global map = correct shapes, wrong policy, broken RL
```

## Break #3: routing drift on off-distribution RL data

MoE training relies on a **load-balancing auxiliary loss** to keep the router
from collapsing onto a few favorite experts. The usual form penalizes the
product of the fraction of tokens routed to each expert and the router's average
probability mass on it:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha \cdot N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \cdot p_i
$$

where $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert $i$ and $p_i$ is the
mean gate probability for it. The product form is deliberate: $f_i$ is a hard
count and non-differentiable, so the gradient flows only through the soft $p_i$
while $f_i$ acts as a detached weighting. Minimized, this pushes the router
toward roughly uniform utilization, so no expert is overloaded and none starves.
It was tuned during pretraining, on the pretraining distribution.

RL post-training does not feed that distribution. The data is **self-generated**:
completions sampled from the current policy, filtered or weighted by reward.
Reward chases a narrow slice of behavior — a particular format, a tool-call
style, a domain — and the token distribution shifts with it. Two failure modes
follow. The router can drift so that the policy's new behavior concentrates on a
handful of experts, leaving capacity idle. Or, if you keep the aux loss strong,
it fights the reward signal — forcing balance the data no longer wants and
blunting the very behavior you are trying to shape. Tuning $\alpha$ against the
reward objective is a knob dense RL never had to touch.

## Break #4: attention-only LoRA underfits an MoE

Parameter-efficient tuning is attractive in RL — full fine-tuning of a giant
policy is expensive, and LoRA on the attention projections is the reflexive
default carried over from dense models. On an MoE that default quietly
underfits, and the reason is just the parameter accounting from the top of the
post.

In a dense transformer, attention and FFN are within the same order of magnitude
— the FFN is the larger slice but not overwhelmingly so — so attention-only LoRA
still touches a meaningful share. In an MoE, the **experts hold the overwhelming
majority of the parameters** — the model is mostly FFN, replicated $N$ times.
The router decides *which* behavior fires and the experts *are* that behavior.
Freeze every expert and adapt only attention, and you are reshaping a thin shell
around a frozen core:

$$
P_{\text{attn}} \ll P_{\text{experts}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{attention-only LoRA leaves most capacity untouched}
$$

If RL needs to move the policy's behavior more than marginally, the adapters
have to reach the experts and ideally the router as well — expert LoRA, or
selectively unfreezing experts — rather than stopping at the attention
projections. The dense intuition that "LoRA on attention is usually enough"
does not transport.

## So what

An MoE is not a drop-in for a dense policy in an RL trainer. The single property
that makes it efficient — huge total parameters, sparse active ones — propagates
into four distinct places:

- **Memory:** experts do not fit replicated; **expert parallelism** is mandatory
  and adds all-to-all routing communication that dense models never pay.
- **Rollout:** the inference engine must implement MoE routing too, and its
  expert-layout mapping must agree with the trainer's or checkpoint conversion
  silently corrupts the policy.
- **Routing:** the load-balancing aux loss was tuned on the pretraining
  distribution; off-distribution RL data can skew routing, and the aux loss can
  fight the reward signal.
- **PEFT:** attention-only LoRA underfits, because the capacity — and the
  behavior RL is trying to shape — lives in the experts.

The throughline is to stop thinking of the MoE as one model and start thinking
of it as $N$ experts plus a router that must stay consistent across training and
inference, balanced against a reward signal that is actively trying to unbalance
it. Get the expert bookkeeping right and the sparsity is still a gift; get it
wrong and the failures are quiet, which is the worst kind.
