Train a model and people obsess over parameter count. Then you go to *serve* it
at long context with a healthy batch size, and a different number quietly eats
all your HBM: the **KV cache**. It scales with sequence length and batch in a way
the weights never do, and on a long-context decode workload it can dwarf the
model itself. Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) is the standard answer — and it's a
nice case study in how a one-line idea ("share the K/V heads") ripples straight
into your fused projection layout and your attention kernel.

## What the KV cache actually is

During autoregressive decode, every new token attends to *every previous token*.
The keys and values for those previous tokens don't change, so you compute them
once and keep them. That stored tensor — per layer, per attention head, over the
whole sequence so far — is the KV cache. It's pure throughput optimization:
without it, generating each new token means re-running the forward pass over the
entire prefix, and the self-attention in that pass is $O(n^2)$ per step.
The cache cuts that to $O(n)$ per step — the new token computes only its own
query, then attends to the $n$ stored keys and values. Over a full $n$-token
generation, that's the difference between $O(n^3)$ and $O(n^2)$ total.

The catch is memory. Across a batch, the cache size is:

$$
\text{bytes} \;=\; 2 \cdot L \cdot H_{kv} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot S \cdot B \cdot b_{\text{dtype}}
$$

where $L$ is the number of layers, $H_{kv}$ the number of **key/value** heads,
$d_{\text{head}}$ the per-head dimension, $S$ the sequence length, $B$ the batch
size, and $b_{\text{dtype}}$ the bytes per element (2 for fp16/bf16). The leading
$2$ is the part everyone forgets: you store **both** K and V.

Plug in real numbers. A 32-layer model, 32 heads, $d_{\text{head}}=128$, fp16,
at 8K context and batch 16:

$$
2 \cdot 32 \cdot 32 \cdot 128 \cdot 8192 \cdot 16 \cdot 2 \;\approx\; 68.7\ \text{GB}
$$

That is *just the cache* — no weights, no activations. The figure is in decimal
gigabytes ($10^9$ bytes), which works out to exactly 64 GiB; an 80 GB-class
device (A100/H100) exposes about 80 GiB, so the cache alone eats roughly 80% of
the memory, leaving little room for weights and activations. And notice what it scales with: linearly in both $S$ and
$B$. Double the context, double the cache; double the batch, double the cache. The
weights, meanwhile, sit at a fixed size no matter how long or how many sequences
you serve. This is why at serving time the KV cache, not the parameter count, is
usually what caps your batch size and your maximum context.

## The lever: there are too many K/V heads

Look back at the formula. $L$, $S$, $B$ are fixed by your model and workload.
$d_{\text{head}}$ is baked into the architecture. The one term you can attack
without retraining a different-shaped network is $H_{kv}$ — the number of *key/
value* heads.

In standard **Multi-Head Attention (MHA)**, every query head gets its own
dedicated K/V head, so $H_{kv} = H_q = H$. That's the leftmost group in the
animation: a clean 1:1 pairing. It's also the expensive end — the cache carries
a full set of K and V for all $H$ heads.

The observation behind the whole family of variants: at inference, the K and V
projections are far less sensitive to head diversity than Q is. You can share
them aggressively and lose surprisingly little.

**Multi-Query Attention (MQA)** takes this to the limit (Shazeer, 2019): a
*single* K/V head shared across **all** query heads. So $H_{kv} = 1$, and the
cache shrinks by a factor of $H$. At our example that's 68.7 GB down to about
2.1 GB — enormous. The cost is quality: collapsing all key/value information into
one head removes representational capacity, and the GQA paper and follow-on work
reported quality degradation and, in some setups, training instability with MQA.

**Grouped-Query Attention (GQA)** is the middle ground (Ainslie et al., 2023).
Partition the $H$ query heads into $G$ groups; each group shares one K/V head. So
$H_{kv} = G$, with $1 \le G \le H$:

$$
G = H \;\Rightarrow\; \text{MHA}, \qquad G = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{MQA}
$$

The cache shrinks by a factor of $H / G$. Pick $G = 8$ for $H = 32$ and the cache
drops 4× — our running example goes from 68.7 GB down to about 17 GB — while
quality stays within noise of full MHA. That's the sweet spot most modern open
models ship with — enough K/V diversity to keep quality, few enough K/V heads to
make long-context serving tractable. The middle group in the animation is
exactly this: a few query heads fanning into each shared K/V head.

A useful detail from the paper: you don't have to pretrain a GQA model from
scratch. You can **uptrain** from an existing MHA checkpoint — mean-pool the K and
V projection weights within each group to initialize the shared head, then
fine-tune on a small fraction of the original compute. The grouped model recovers
nearly all the quality for a fraction of the cost.

## The systems catch: now Q, K, and V are different widths

Here's where the "just share the K/V heads" idea stops being one line of math and
starts touching real code. In MHA, the three projections are symmetric: Q, K, and
V each produce $H \cdot d_{\text{head}}$ features. That symmetry is why
implementations love a **fused QKV projection** — one big matmul that emits all
three at once, then a clean reshape into three equal `[H, d_head]` blocks.

GQA breaks the symmetry. Q still has $H$ heads, but K and V now have only $G$. The
fused weight is no longer three equal slabs:

$$
W_{qkv} \in \mathbb{R}^{\,d_{\text{model}} \;\times\; (\,H + 2G\,)\,d_{\text{head}}}
$$

The output of that matmul has to be split **unevenly**: the first
$H \cdot d_{\text{head}}$ columns are Q, the next $G \cdot d_{\text{head}}$ are K,
the last $G \cdot d_{\text{head}}$ are V. Any code that assumed three equal thirds
— weight-loading, tensor-parallel sharding, the reshape after the projection —
has to be taught the new layout. This is the most common place GQA integrations
break: a checkpoint converter or a TP split that still slices the fused weight
into three equal pieces will silently scramble heads.

```python
# illustrative — not a real implementation
# fused QKV with GQA: Q has H heads, K and V have G heads each
qkv = x @ W_qkv                       # [B, S, (H + 2G) * d_head]
q, k, v = qkv.split(
    [H * d_head, G * d_head, G * d_head], dim=-1
)
q = q.view(B, S, H, d_head)           # H query heads
k = k.view(B, S, G, d_head)           # G key heads
v = v.view(B, S, G, d_head)           # G value heads
```

Under tensor parallelism it gets fussier still. With $N$ TP ranks you want each
rank to own $H/N$ query heads **and** the matching $G/N$ K/V heads, so that a
group never straddles two devices. The clean partition requires $G$ divisible by
$N$; when $N > G$, implementations replicate the K/V head across ranks instead.
That's a constraint that doesn't exist for MHA and that quietly limits which
parallelism configurations are legal.

## And the attention kernel has to broadcast

The second half of the integration is the attention computation itself. Each
query head needs a key/value head to attend against. In MHA the pairing is
trivial: head $i$ uses K/V head $i$. In GQA, query head $i$ must use K/V head
$\lfloor i / (H/G) \rfloor$ — its group's shared head. Several query heads now
point at the **same** K and V.

The naive way to make existing kernels work is to physically replicate each K/V
head $H/G$ times so the tensors line up at $H$ heads again, then run ordinary
MHA. That's correct, and it's what a `repeat_kv` helper does — but it throws away
the entire memory win at compute time by re-materializing the full-size K and V.

```python
# illustrative — not a real implementation
def repeat_kv(x, n_rep):                  # x: [B, S, G, d_head]
    B, S, G, d = x.shape
    if n_rep == 1:
        return x
    return (x[:, :, :, None, :]            # [B, S, G, 1, d]
              .expand(B, S, G, n_rep, d)   # broadcast across the group
              .reshape(B, S, G * n_rep, d))  # back to H heads
```

A real fused kernel does better. FlashAttention and the serving engines don't
replicate; they **index**. Each query head reads its group's single K/V head
directly from the small cache, so the cache stays at $G$ heads in memory and the
group structure is just an index map inside the kernel. This is the right mental
model: GQA's saving is real only if your attention path *reads* the shared head
in place rather than expanding it back out. A correct-but-slow integration
replicates; a good one indexes.

## So what

GQA is a tiny architectural idea with an outsized payoff, and the reason it
matters is entirely about serving, not training accuracy:

- The KV cache scales as $2 \cdot L \cdot H_{kv} \cdot d_{\text{head}} \cdot S \cdot B \cdot b_{\text{dtype}}$, linear in sequence length and batch — so at long context it, not the weights, sets your memory ceiling.
- $H_{kv}$ is the one term you can shrink. MHA sets $H_{kv} = H$, MQA sets it to $1$, GQA sets it to $G$ and cuts the cache by $H/G$ while keeping quality near MHA.
- The cost lands in the plumbing: the fused QKV weight becomes three *unequal* slabs of width $(H + 2G)\,d_{\text{head}}$, the clean tensor-parallel split wants $G$ divisible by the TP degree (replicating the K/V head when ranks outnumber it), and the attention kernel must index each shared K/V head across its group rather than replicate it.

Get the head bookkeeping right in both the projection split and the kernel, and
you turn a model whose cache devours an 80 GB GPU at 8K context into one that
comfortably serves long sequences at a useful batch size — same parameters, a
fraction of the cache.
