If you profile a synchronous RL post-training run, the first thing that jumps
out is how *little* of the time the GPUs are actually training. They spend most
of it waiting. This post is about where that wasted time comes from, and the
ladder of scheduling techniques that claw it back — **interleaving**,
**pipelining**, and **full async** — plus the second, orthogonal question of
*where* rollout and training physically run.

The two ideas are easy to conflate, so I'll keep them on separate axes:

- **Policy freshness** — how stale is the data the trainer learns from? This is
  the ladder: synchronous → interleaved → pipelined → fully async.
- **Placement** — do rollout and training share the same GPUs (colocated), or
  run on separate fleets (separate-stage)?

The animation below is the freshness ladder. We'll walk each rung, then come
back to placement.

## The RL post-training loop, and its bubble

One step of policy-gradient RL post-training (PPO, GRPO, RLOO, DAPO — the family
is large, the loop is the same) is three stages:

1. **Rollout / generation** — the current policy generates completions for a
   batch of prompts. Autoregressive decode, usually on vLLM or SGLang.
2. **Reward / scoring** — a reward model, verifier, or rule scores them.
3. **Training** — compute advantages, take a gradient step on the policy.

In the **fully synchronous** version these run strictly in order, and step 3
can't begin until step 1 has *completely* finished. The problem is the
distribution of generation lengths.

Generation time is dominated by the **longest sequence in the batch**. Decoding
is autoregressive, so a completion that runs to 4,000 tokens takes roughly 8× as
long as one that stops at 500 — and they're in the *same* batch. The trainer
sits idle until the very last straggler emits its final token.

$$
T_{\text{rollout}} \;\approx\; \max_{i \in \text{batch}} \, t_i
\qquad\text{not}\qquad
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} t_i
$$

That gap — trainer GPUs powered on, allocated, doing nothing — is the **bubble**.
In long-context RL with high length variance, the bubble can be the majority of
wall-clock time. The data, at least, is maximally fresh: every sample was
generated by exactly the policy you're about to update. **Staleness is zero.**
We'll spend the rest of the post trading some of that freshness for throughput.

## Rung 1: interleaved rollout (still on-policy)

The first rung looks like it should introduce staleness — but it doesn't, and
*why* is the important part.

Split the global batch into **mini-batches**. Now overlap the *generation* of
later mini-batches with the *training* (forward/backward + gradient
accumulation) of earlier ones. The two lanes run concurrently, so the bubble
shrinks.

The key: **the optimizer step — the actual weight update — fires only after
*all* mini-batches in the global batch have finished training.** Until that
barrier, the policy weights never change. So every mini-batch in the batch,
whether generated first or last, came from the *same* policy version.

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}
\qquad\text{once per global batch, after all mini-batches}
$$

That's why interleaved rollout is **still on-policy, staleness 0**. In the
animation it's the regime with the dashed **Δθ** markers — the only points where
weights update. Everything between two Δθ lines was generated by one frozen
policy. You've overlapped the work without changing the math of the update at
all.

This is the safest throughput win there is — no algorithmic change, no staleness
to manage — but it's bounded: gen and train still both finish before the
barrier, so if generation is much slower than training (it usually is in
long-context RL), the trainer still waits.

## Rung 2: pipelined async (one step off-policy)

To overlap *more*, drop the per-batch barrier and let the timelines slide by one
step: while the trainer does step $k$, the rollout engine is already generating
the batch that step $k{+}1$ will consume.

```python
# illustrative one-step pipeline
prev = rollout.generate(policy)            # warm up: batch for step 0
for k in range(max_steps):
    next_future = rollout.generate_async(policy)  # gen batch k+1 ...
    trainer.step(prev)                            # ... while training on batch k
    policy = trainer.weights
    prev = next_future.result()
```

Now the lanes overlap steadily — but notice batch $k{+}1$ was generated by the
policy from *before* step $k$'s update. The data is exactly **one step
off-policy**. That's a small, fixed amount of staleness, usually harmless and
sometimes even mildly regularizing. Utilization climbs again; the bubble is
mostly gone.

## Rung 3: fully async (bounded staleness)

The top rung removes the lockstep entirely. Generators run **continuously**,
never waiting for a training step; the trainer consumes whatever completions are
ready whenever it's ready. Neither lane blocks the other, so utilization
approaches 100% — both lanes packed solid in the animation.

The price is that a trajectory might be generated by the policy at step $k$ but
used at step $k{+}3$. The data is now **multi-step off-policy**, and unbounded
staleness destabilizes training. So the real engineering is *bounding* it:

$$
\text{staleness}(i) \;=\; k_{\text{train}} - k_{\text{gen}}(i) \;\le\; s_{\max}
$$

and correcting for policy drift with the same per-token importance-sampling
ratio PPO already uses, applied and clipped token-by-token so the surrogate
keeps the off-policy update in check:

$$
r_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{gen}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}
$$

Tune $s_{\max}$ too tight and you're back to waiting; too loose and you trade
throughput for instability. Finding that knee — for a given model size, context
length, and reward landscape — is most of the actual work.

## The other axis: where does it all run?

Everything above is the *freshness* axis. Orthogonal to it is **placement** —
which GPUs do rollout and training actually use? This is where a detail trips
people up, so it's worth stating plainly.

- **Colocated (hybrid engine):** trainer and rollout share the same GPUs,
  time-slicing them. Memory-efficient, but the two stages can't truly run at the
  same instant on the same device.
- **Separate-stage:** rollout runs on one fleet, training on another. They can
  run genuinely concurrently, at the cost of holding two copies of the model and
  moving weights between fleets.

The two axes interact in a way worth memorizing:

| Freshness regime | Colocated hybrid engine | Separate-stage |
| --- | :---: | :---: |
| Fully synchronous | ✅ | ✅ |
| Interleaved | — | ✅ |
| Pipelined | — | ✅ |
| Fully async | ✅ *(via partial rollout)* | ✅ |

**Interleaved and pipelined are separate-stage only.** Both depend on gen and
train running *concurrently* — and on colocated GPUs, where the trainer and the
rollout engine share the same devices, you can't. Time-slicing the shared GPUs
doesn't rescue it: run the slices back-to-back and there's no overlap (that's
just synchronous), or pause generation and resume it after the training step and
the resumed trajectories are now from a stale policy (that's async, with
staleness). The instant you share devices you lose either the overlap or the
on-policy property — so true interleaving needs gen and train on separate
fleets.

**Fully async can be reached either way**, and the colocated path is the
interesting one. On shared GPUs you can't just let generation run forever — the
trainer needs those same devices. So you use **partial rollout**: when it's time
to train, you *pause* the in-flight generations (checkpoint their KV state),
hand the GPUs to the trainer for a step, then *resume* the paused trajectories
where they left off.

```python
# illustrative colocated async via partial rollout
inflight = engine.submit(oversampled_prompts())
for step in range(max_steps):
    done, inflight = engine.harvest(target=batch_size)  # fastest-first
    paused = engine.pause(inflight)        # checkpoint KV of the long tail
    trainer.step(assemble(done))           # GPUs now free for training
    engine.resume(paused)                  # continue where they left off
```

Partial rollout is not itself a freshness regime — it's the *mechanism* that
makes fully-async training possible **on colocated hardware**, by ensuring no
single long-tail trajectory monopolizes the shared GPUs. (Separate-stage fleets
get async from running independently, so they don't need partial rollout to free
GPUs for the trainer — though they may still interrupt long-tail generations to
bound staleness.)

## So, where do the bubbles go?

They don't *go* anywhere — you stop creating them, one rung at a time:

- **Interleaved** overlaps mini-batch gen and train, with the weight update held
  to the global-batch barrier — **on-policy, zero staleness.**
- **Pipelined** slides the timelines by one step — **one step off-policy.**
- **Fully async** unlocks the lanes entirely — **bounded staleness**, paid with
  a carry cap and importance weighting.
- **Placement** decides *how* you get there: separate-stage fleets run
  concurrently by construction; colocated hybrid engines reach full async
  through **partial rollout**.

Climb the ladder and the idle hatching in that opening animation collapses from
"half the timeline" to "almost nothing" — same hardware, far more useful work —
as long as you keep the staleness you've taken on under control.
